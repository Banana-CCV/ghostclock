## Three most pitch-worthy cases

1. **Croydon gun-supply trial (2012, Met Police ENF).** The defence said the undercover recording of an arms deal was spliced together. The Met's ENF expert matched the mains hum to National Grid records, which put it at the claimed date and time and showed it was one continuous take. The defendants were convicted (33 years in total). This is GhostClock's exact technique in a real British courtroom. Caveat: the ENF detail comes from reputable secondary write-ups, not from a law report.
2. **Maldives, Shameem hammer-attack acquittal (2025).** Seven defendants were acquitted because CCTV timestamps from different cameras did not line up with the attack time, and the judge called it a "break in sequence" in the evidence. An independent clock taken from the grid, applied to all the footage, is precisely what was missing. The case is under appeal.
3. **UK family-court doctored audio (Byron James, 2019-20).** A parent filed an edited recording of the father "threatening" her to block his access to the children. It was exposed only because his lawyers got the original file and its metadata showed post-call edits. GhostClock's splice detection tests the signal itself, so it still works when metadata has been stripped or forged. Pair this with Waiwai (NZ 2016), where an alibi failed on a 4.5-minute CCTV clock error, for "seconds matter".

**Q&A guard-rails:** (a) ENF case law is mostly trial-level and press-reported. Say "used in UK criminal trials since about 2010", not "approved by appellate courts". (b) The MoJ reform deliberately leaves out device recordings, so authenticity of recordings stays a matter for evidence and experts, which is GhostClock's space. (c) ENF needs mains hum in the recording. Battery-only outdoor recordings may carry no ENF.
